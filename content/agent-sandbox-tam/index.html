1. Market definition and method

The model is a bottom-up demand chain. For each demand segment and cohort, in each year:

tasks = eligible_organizations × adoption_share × annual_tasks_per_adopter
runs = tasks × environment_required_share × environment_runs_per_task
resource_demand[r] = runs × resource_units_per_run[r]
economic_TAM = Σ resource_demand[r] × comparable_service_price[r] + separately priced installed base and platform fees

  • Adoption applies to eligible organizations; the environment requirement applies to tasks. Runs per task captures retries and parallel branches; duration is per environment run.
  • Four demand segments: coding agents, business workflows, browser/desktop computer use, and RL/evaluation. Runtime configurations (light interpreter, short execution, browser session, GPU eval) are resource mixes within segments, not additional populations — so no double counting of customers.
  • Billing-basis caveat: vendors bill either allocated time or active CPU. For the same workload this moves the price 2.3× (short runs) to 3.01× (long-lived months). The compute price states its basis; a duty-cycle converter is provided in section 5.
  • Installed base uses its own denominator (adopting organizations), never multiplied by tasks. Browser-in-VM compute counts once; separately billed proxy traffic is the one deliberate addition. RL GPU inference outside the environment layer is excluded.
  • Market outputs: economic TAM (all qualifying demand at stated comparable service prices, including internal/self-hosted equivalents), merchant market (hosted demand at realized prices plus platform fees on BYOC/self-hosted), provisional product SAM (merchant × workload × region × deployment filters; labeled provisional because product scope is not fixed), and SOM (reachable revenue = SAM × reachable share × conversion).
  • List price and realized price are distinct. All collected prices are vendor list prices; realized prices after credits and discounts are unobserved except two customer datapoints.

Scenario semantics: low = narrow survey definitions, floor prices, low outsourcing; mid = central anchors; high = broad definitions, ceiling prices, heavy task rates. The engine is a faithful browser port of the reference implementation (same formulas, same scenario semantics); the input ledger is embedded below in sanitized form — values, units, and provenance classes only.

Provenance labels and record kinds (methodology note)

Status badges use eight provenance classes only: primary source, customer interview, secondary reporting, derived estimate, modeling assumption, conflicting, stale, unverified. A class describes how an input is supported, not the underlying source, which is omitted from this public edition.

Record kinds are not statuses. In the assumptions ledger, kind: observation, kind: gap, kind: reconciliation_target, and kind: conflict_map mark what a row is (a calibration anchor, a null gap, a legacy target, a mapping note). They are rendered as a distinct double-bordered badge and never as a provenance class; rows without a kind badge are model inputs.

Concise conclusion (mid-case defaults). The central case puts the 2026 merchant market at $1.32B — inside the legacy $0.65B–$1.35B band, near its top — and the 2030 economic TAM at $25.4B, at the top edge of the legacy $10B–$25B band. Uncertainty around those points is dominated by organization counts, task rates per adopter, and realized prices — not by vendor list prices, which two independent derivations anchor to within rounding. The low and high cases are bounds: the high case stacks ceiling values on purpose (see the sensitivity section before quoting it), and the low case cannot reproduce the two vendor-disclosed 2026 run rates, which is itself an unresolved method disagreement shown in section 9.

8.4 Limitations and research priorities (summary)

The sourced internal edition carries a full ranked gap ledger and a conflict register with both sides of every discrepancy. This public summary states the substance without record ids. Each item corresponds to a null-valued gap record (G-01…G-10) in the assumptions table.

  1. Organization counts are missing. No organization-level population counts exist for the browser/desktop segment, agent RL and evaluation teams, or software-developing organizations generally. The eligible-organizations driver is assumption-led for two of four segments; this is the largest single source of TAM variance.
  2. All prices are list prices. Realized prices after discounts and credits are unobserved except two customer datapoints, so the realized-price factor is an explicit assumption.
  3. Free-tier share is unknown. Published cumulative usage counts mix paid and free traffic, so volume counts cannot anchor revenue without a paid share.
  4. Revenue often appears as growth multiples without bases. Four providers disclose only growth multiples, which cannot anchor a market size.
  5. Some platform rates are unpublished. Three major platform services lack primary published session rates, so that layer is understated here.
  6. Customer spend anchors are single-customer. Every demand-side spend anchor rests on one customer; there is no broad spend sample.
  7. Adoption series break across definitions. Survey definitions changed over 2025–2026 and differ by region and weighting; adoption varies about 2× by definition choice. Scenarios anchor to one definition each rather than splicing series.
  8. Browser per-session economics need assumptions. Memory shape, proxy traffic per session, and plan-versus-overage mixing leave the browser segment price uncertain within about 2×.
  9. Provider status can change quickly. Entrants and exits move the top of the price range; top-of-range list prices should be re-checked before reuse.
  10. Self-reported figures are claims, not audits. Vendor revenue figures are vendor-reported run rates or annualized ARR, never audited recognized revenue. Cumulative starts are not annual starts.

Divergence drivers

  1. The scenario spread itself is the largest divergence. 2026 merchant spans $44M (low) to $32.1B (high) around a $1.32B mid. The high side stacks the coding task-rate ceiling (80K tasks/adopter/year — no broad customer spend sample exists) on top-of-band org conversion and adoption. Read high as a bound; the OAT sensitivities in section 6 decompose the bundle.
  2. Low 2026 merchant sits ~10× below two vendor-disclosed run rates alone (Modal $300M + E2B >$100M, annualized, Oct–Nov 2026). Narrow survey-style demand definitions cannot reproduce observed vendor revenue: either they undercount agent execution, or vendor run rates cover demand broader than the canonical sandbox. Both readings stay on the record.
  3. Low 2030 merchant ($253M) is ~14× below the legacy low ($3.5B): floor prices plus adoption paths that reach only 30% (coding) and 8% (workflows) by 2030.
  4. Proxy traffic is material: at mid assumptions, residential proxy adds about $0.04 per browser session, about 3× the browser runtime fee. Per-session traffic (0.005 GB) is an assumption, so the browser segment's absolute level is soft.
  5. RL/evaluation GPU economics are the least evidenced value chain: no primary GPU-environment rate exists in the underlying research; A-PRICE-GPU-H is a flagged placeholder.

RSI exploratory scenario (separate toggle, not a forecast)

A speculative upside narrative from earlier internal work is kept separate from the central forecast: separately enabled, explicit mechanisms, no claimed probability. The toggle changes no model input; it only shows the narrative's bands next to the model outputs.

Mechanisms claimed by the narrative (each is a claim in that narrative, not an observation in the underlying research):

  1. Compute-to-human decoupling: a single frontier lab or autonomous engineering loop can spawn tens of thousands of concurrent sandboxes per second (the narrative cites Modal at >10,000 creations/sec, 1M+ concurrent instances), so seat counts stop capping consumption.
  2. Shift from inference tokens to environment execution: multi-step verification (compile, test, browse, self-correct) moves the bill from token generation to environment compute at roughly $0.17–$0.24 per 2-vCPU sandbox-hour.
  3. Mandatory security boundaries: 2026 sandbox-escape incidents push enterprises from shared-kernel containers to hardware-isolated microVMs and egress proxies, making isolation a compliance primitive rather than a dev tool.

What each legacy hypothesis would have required

  • ~$0.95B 2026 merchant (prior internal estimate, range $0.65B–$1.35B): 0.72× the mid case. It needs the realized-price factor at 0.47 instead of 0.65, or coding-internal adoption at 11% instead of 15%. Model mid ($1.32B) lands inside the legacy range, near its top.
  • ~$7B 2030 merchant (range $3.5B–$12.5B): 0.67× mid. It needs 2030 coding adoption at 33% instead of 50%, or 2030 tasks per internal adopter at 67K instead of 100K. Model mid ($10.5B) is inside the range.
  • ~$17B 2030 economic TAM (range $10B–$25B): 0.67× mid. Model mid ($25.4B) sits at the top edge of the legacy band.
  • Calibration, not curve-fitting: the platform-builder cohort was calibrated once against vendor-disclosed Modal ($300M) + E2B (>$100M) run rates — evidence, not a legacy target. Modal + E2B equal about 30% of mid 2026 merchant, consistent with two dominant vendors in a 60+ provider universe.
