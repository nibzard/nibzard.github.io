Read this before you quote a number

  • The mid case is the central estimate. Low and high are bounds, not a confidence interval.
  • Treat the high case as a ceiling. It sets many inputs to their highest values at the same time. That is unlikely.
  • Total demand is not revenue. It includes work that companies run on their own servers. Vendor revenue is the part that vendors can bill.
  • All prices are list prices, reduced by a discount assumption. Real discounts are not public.
  • Read 2026 as an end-of-year run rate. The vendors that report numbers grew 12–37× in 2026, so their calendar-year revenue is only about 30–50% of their year-end run rate.
  • Big buyers can build in-house. If buyers that make up 30% of vendor-hosted demand move it to their own servers by 2030, mid vendor revenue for 2030 falls by nearly 30%. See the edge case on the Check tab.
  • Personal agents add a lot of demand but little vendor revenue. Meta, OpenAI, Google, and xAI run them on their own servers. The model assumes 10–15% reaches third-party sandbox vendors in the mid case.

How the number is calculated

The model counts work, not companies. It starts with organizations, estimates how many agent tasks they run, and prices the sandbox time those tasks use. Personal agents are counted by person and added at step 7.

Rules that prevent double counting
  • Adoption applies to organizations. The sandbox share applies to tasks. Each multiplier has one base.
  • Runs per task include retries and parallel attempts. The model does not multiply by concurrency again.
  • Runtime types are a mix inside each segment, not extra customers. A run is a light interpreter call, a short VM run, a browser session, or a GPU evaluation run.
  • Persistent computers, stored data, and platform fees use adopting organizations as their base, not task counts.
  • A browser that runs inside a VM counts once. Proxy traffic that is billed separately is added.
  • Excluded: AI model token costs, ordinary model training and inference, generic web hosting, and orchestration-only software.
  • Billing basis matters. Some vendors bill for allocated time, others for active CPU time. For the same workload, this changes the price by 2.3× (short runs) to 3× (long-lived machines).
The formulas
tasks = organizations × adoption × tasks per adopter runs = tasks × sandbox share × runs per task demand = runs × resources per run (vCPU-hours, GiB-hours, ...) total demand = Σ demand × list price + persistent computers + stored data + platform fees vendor revenue = hosted share × (compute + computers + storage) × discount factor + platform fees reachable SAM = vendor revenue × workload × region × deployment fit winnable SOM = SAM × reachable share × win rate

    Who builds in-house

    AI labs and consumer platforms own their sandbox layer. OpenAI agreed to buy Ona (formerly Gitpod) for Codex cloud environments[4]. Meta owns Manus, a former E2B customer. xAI Grok Bot runs on Cursor, which SpaceX owns. This is the real risk to vendors: a few very large buyers.

    Companies that build internal agents for their staff are a different case. One public catalog lists 53 internal agents at 42 companies, mostly tech companies[5]. An in-house agent does not mean an in-house sandbox:

    • Rents a vendor: Ramp runs each Inspect session in a Modal sandbox[6].
    • Reuses its own developer machines: Stripe runs Minions on its own cloud devboxes[7].
    • Builds its own isolation: DoorDash Flux uses Firecracker microVMs[8]. Snap Casper uses disposable sandboxes[9].

    The model already puts 20% of organization demand on the buyer's own servers in the mid case, and 82–87% of personal-agent demand. This edge case moves more on top of that.

    Model labs sell sandboxes too

    Anthropic and OpenAI now run agents for developers, sandbox included. Both also let you bring your own sandbox.

    • Claude Managed Agents (Anthropic): tokens plus $0.08 per session-hour, counted only while the agent runs. The container is included[11]. Or run the tools in your own sandbox: Anthropic lists guides for 11 providers, including E2B, Modal, Daytona, Cloudflare, Vercel, and AWS[12].
    • OpenAI Agents API: runs the Codex harness. Use an OpenAI-hosted sandbox, your own, or one of nine partners, including E2B, Modal, Daytona, and Runloop[13]. Third-party reports put a 4 GB container at $0.12 per 20 minutes.

    Price per hour

    What this changes in the model: no new demand, because these agents already count in the coding and business-workflow segments. A lab that bills for sandbox time counts as a vendor. The effects are on price and on who gets paid. Bundled runtime below list price pushes prices down. Lists of interchangeable providers send volume to independent vendors but cut their margins.

    Where the model is weak

    • The scenario spread is very wide. The high case sets many inputs to their maximum at the same time. Use it as a ceiling only.
    • The low case is set by reported revenue, not measured. The old low case gave $0.04B for 2026, far below what three vendors report. Every low input now sits two-thirds of the way to mid. That makes 2026 low vendor revenue about equal to the three reported run rates, as if every other vendor earned nothing.
    • Three vendors report, and two of those numbers are self-reported projections or headlines. Their share of the market is a guess.
    • The RL/evaluation size rests on one vendor's statement. Daytona's CEO says about 70% of its revenue comes from training agents[10]. Modal and E2B publish no split. RL task volumes were tripled to match. Labs could also bring this work in-house.
    • List prices stay flat to 2030 by default. Free clones, cheap open-source browsers, and lab bundles all push prices down. Use the yearly price change on the Experiment tab to test it.
    • Organization counts and tasks per organization are estimates. No public data counts them directly. These inputs drive most of the uncertainty.
    • Real prices are unknown. All prices are list prices. The discount factor is an assumption.
    • Browser proxy traffic is a large cost. At mid values it costs about 3× the browser runtime. Traffic per session is an assumption.
    • GPU evaluation prices have no direct source. The RL/evaluation segment rests on a placeholder price.
    • Personal agents are new and thinly measured. They launched between May and October 2026. Only Meta publishes user numbers (more than 10 million setups). The price of a personal-agent computer depends on how long it stays awake, and no provider discloses that.
    Upside scenario: self-improving AI (not a forecast)

    An earlier narrative argued for a much larger market if AI systems improve themselves. It does not change any model input. It names three mechanisms:

    1. Compute grows faster than headcount. One automated engineering loop can start thousands of sandboxes per second, so the number of people no longer limits use.
    2. Spend moves from tokens to execution. Agents compile, test, and browse to check their own work. That bill is sandbox time.
    3. Isolation becomes mandatory. Security incidents push companies from shared containers to hardware-isolated VMs.

    In scope: isolated execution environments (sandboxes) for coding agents, business workflow agents, browser and desktop use by agents, agent training and evaluation (RL/evaluation), and always-on personal agents.

    Personal agents count people, not organizations. The main cost is one cloud computer per person, priced per user-month while it is awake. Tasks inside that computer are not counted again. AI subscription fees are model revenue and are out of scope.

    Total demand (economic TAM): all qualifying sandbox use, valued at market list prices. It includes work that companies run on their own infrastructure. It is not money that anybody spends today.

    Vendor revenue (merchant market): the part that outside vendors can bill. Hosted use at discounted prices, plus software fees when customers run sandboxes in their own cloud.

    Reachable SAM: vendor revenue that a typical new product could serve, given its workloads, regions, and deployment options. It is provisional because no product scope is fixed.

    Winnable SOM: the part of SAM that one company can reach and win.

    Scenarios. Low: narrow adoption definitions, small task volumes, lowest prices. Mid: central values. High: broad definitions, heavy use, highest prices.
